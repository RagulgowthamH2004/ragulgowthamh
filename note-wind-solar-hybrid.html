Feasibility framing for hybrid wind–solar generation

Subject Hybrid renewable feasibilityContext Resource, grid, financial and risk analysisLength 12 min read

A wind–solar hybrid combines solar PV and wind generation on one site, often with battery storage, on the premise that two resources with different generation patterns can complement each other. The premise is sound. The failure mode is assuming it rather than measuring it.

Why combine them

Solar output follows a fixed daily shape: high in daylight, zero at night. Wind follows wind speed, which in some locations is stronger precisely when solar is weak. A recent Indian hybrid mini-grid study found wind availability through June to August compensating for weaker monsoon-season solar at the site studied.

Complementarity is a property of a specific site. It has to be measured there, not inherited from a paper about somewhere else.

The first mistake

Most feasibility studies open with “how many MW of solar and wind can we install?” That question is premature, and asking it first quietly fixes the answer to every question that follows.

Start instead with what problem are we solving? Low renewable availability at night, intermittency, grid support, peak demand, firm renewable supply, land optimisation, transmission utilisation, or a procurement obligation. The business requirement determines the technical design. Reversing that order is how projects end up technically elegant and commercially pointless.

Resource assessment

Solar

Global horizontal irradiance, plane-of-array irradiance, temperature, cloud cover, seasonal variation, soiling, shading.

Wind

Wind speed and direction, shear, turbulence, air density, seasonal variation, hub-height wind speed.

The question is not whether the site is sunny, nor whether it is windy. It is how the two behave together, over time — which annual averages cannot tell you.

The combined profile is smoother than either component. That smoothing is the entire value proposition of the hybrid, and it is invisible in annual figures — which is why hourly or sub-hourly modelling is worth the effort it costs.

Capacity factor

CF = actual energy generated ÷ maximum possible energy A 100 MW plant could theoretically produce 100 × 8,760 = 876,000 MWh per year. Generating 262,800 MWh gives a capacity factor of 30%.

The technical questions worth answering

Solar

  • Annual solar resource
  • PV technology selected
  • Expected degradation
  • Expected annual generation

Wind

  • Wind speed distribution
  • Appropriate turbine model
  • Expected annual generation
  • Wake losses across the array

Grid

  • Distance to the nearest substation
  • Available evacuation capacity
  • Transmission losses
  • Grid connection requirements

Storage

  • Is a BESS required at all?
  • What capacity, and what duration?
  • Round-trip efficiency
  • Cycle life and degradation

P50, P75 and P90

Generation forecasts carry uncertainty, and the financial model has to carry it too. P50 is the central estimate, P75 more conservative, P90 more conservative still. A lender will often care more about the downside cases than the median, because the debt has to be serviced in a bad year rather than an average one.

Resource uncertainty → generation uncertainty → revenue uncertainty → financing risk.

The indicators

LCOE = lifetime discounted costs ÷ lifetime discounted generation Lower is generally better — but LCOE alone ignores when the electricity arrives, which for a hybrid is most of the point
  • NPV — project value accounting for the time value of money.
  • IRR — the discount rate at which NPV reaches zero.
  • Payback period — how long until cash flows recover the initial investment.

The SWOT belongs at the end

How the SWOT is framed decides which assumptions ever get revisited after financial close, which is why writing it early is actively harmful. A generic one reads: strength, renewable energy; weakness, intermittency; opportunity, government support; threat, grid problems. None of that is about this project.

Generic

Strength: renewable energy. Weakness: intermittency. Opportunity: government support. Threat: grid problems.

Tied to assumptions

Strength: measured wind–solar complementarity at this site. Weakness: design complexity and additional power electronics. Opportunity: hybrid procurement and storage improving the supply profile. Threat: resource uncertainty, curtailment, transmission constraints, tariff change, equipment prices, financing conditions.

Indian context

India operates a National Wind-Solar Hybrid Policy, and MNRE continues to publish guidelines relating to hybrid projects, including 2024 competitive bidding guidelines for procurement of power from grid-connected wind–solar hybrid projects. Regulatory attention increasingly treats hybrids alongside scheduling, transmission and storage rather than as a standalone category.

A workflow that keeps the order honest

  1. Define the objective — what are we actually trying to achieve?
  2. Select the site — land, grid, resource, access
  3. Assess the solar resource — GHI, POA, temperature, losses
  4. Assess the wind resource — speed, direction, turbulence, shear
  5. Analyse complementarity — hourly and monthly correlation
  6. Develop configurations — solar only, solar plus wind, or with BESS
  7. Simulate generation — annual, monthly, hourly
  8. Evaluate the grid — evacuation, curtailment, transmission
  9. Calculate economics — CAPEX, OPEX, revenue, NPV, IRR, LCOE
  10. Run risk analysis — P50, P75, P90 and sensitivities
  11. Write the SWOT — only now, once the assumptions are known
  12. Investment decision — proceed, modify or reject
