Agrivoltaics: what the panel does to the crop underneath

Subject Agrivoltaic systemsContext Land use, shade and crop responseLength 10 min read

Agrivoltaics puts agriculture and photovoltaics on the same land. Rather than choosing between a farm and a solar plant, the system attempts both — which sounds like a free lunch and is not. The whole discipline lives in one question.

Not “do solar panels reduce yield?” but: how much shade, at what time of day, for which crop, under what climate?

What the panel does to the crop

Light

Panels reduce the direct sunlight reaching the canopy, so photosynthetically active radiation falls. For a shade-intolerant crop that is straightforwardly a loss. For a shade-tolerant one, partial shading may be tolerated or occasionally welcome.

Temperature

Panels modify the crop microclimate. On a very hot day, partial shading can reduce thermal stress — a real benefit in an Indian summer. Push the shading too far and photosynthesis is restricted and yield falls. The response is not linear and it is not monotonic.

Soil moisture

Shading alters evaporation, rainfall distribution at ground level, soil moisture and therefore irrigation demand. In hot or water-stressed environments this is often the largest effect, and it is the one most likely to be quoted without its conditions attached.

Wind

The structures change local wind flow. Reduced exposure can lower evaporation and physical stress on some crops, and change disease pressure on others.

Water, carefully

Reduced radiation at the soil surface can cut evaporative losses. What it does not do is deliver “50% less water” for every crop, which is how the benefit is often reported. The actual figure depends on crop, climate, panel density, irrigation method, soil, panel height, orientation, rainfall and season.

Agrivoltaic design is therefore site-specific by necessity, not by preference. This is the same discipline that applies to any water-use claim: the number has to come out of the hydrologic conditions actually present rather than a generic assumption carried over from another site.

Shade is a pattern, not a quantity

The useful mental model is a moving shade pattern rather than a fixed percentage. Morning, midday and afternoon each produce a different geometry, so the crop experiences a changing light environment through the day and across the season. Panel height, tilt, orientation and row spacing are the design levers that control how much shade falls where, and when.

Layouts

Elevated

Panels mounted above the crop, high enough for agricultural machinery to operate underneath.

Inter-row

Crops grown in the gaps between panel rows, with the shade pattern sweeping across them.

Vertical

Near-vertical mounting, freeing the ground between panels for cultivation and machinery.

Tracking

Panels reorienting through the day, which makes shade itself a controllable variable.

ICAR has reported Indian work on agrivoltaic systems including models growing crops beneath solar panels, and an Agri-PV pilot facility at ICAR-IARI.

Land Equivalent Ratio

LER is the metric that makes the case quantitatively. It compares the combined agricultural and solar output of one hectare against the land that would be needed to produce the same outputs separately.

If LER > 1, the combined system yields more useful output per unit of land than the two uses kept apart.Crop contribution plus energy contribution, each expressed against its conventional single-use baseline

ICAR has reported an agrivoltaic model reaching an LER of up to 1.41 under its reported conditions. That is a genuinely strong result and it should not be read as a universal one — it belongs to that crop, that geometry and that site.

Which crops

Suitability turns on shade tolerance, plant height, growing season, water requirement, machinery needs, market value and how yield actually responds to reduced radiation. Vegetables, leafy crops, herbs and some fruits are plausible candidates. Plausible is not the same as demonstrated: a real feasibility study needs local crop trials, because the interaction is between a specific cultivar and a specific light regime.

Benefits and risks, side by side

Where it helps

  • Possible reduction in heat stress and in evaporation
  • Diversified land use and an additional revenue stream for the farm
  • Some protection from weather
  • Decentralised power for irrigation or farm operations

Where it bites

  • Excessive shade and reduced yield
  • Higher structural cost than a ground-mount plant
  • Machinery access, maintenance access and panel cleaning
  • Uneven rainfall distribution beneath the array, and panel soiling
  • Land-use conflict and construction disturbance
  • Crop-specific performance uncertainty

India is actively researching solarisation of agriculture, with irrigation, farm operations and agrivoltaics identified as priority areas at ICAR's 2026 National Forum on Solarization of Agriculture.

The research question, stated properly

Agrivoltaics is not automatically sustainable, and framing it as panels-versus-yield guarantees a shallow answer. The question worth asking is which combination of crop, climate, panel geometry and irrigation strategy produces the best overall outcome — where “overall” has to include energy, yield, water and cost together, because optimising any one of them alone will quietly damage another.
