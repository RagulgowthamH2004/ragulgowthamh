Evaluating Hargreaves–Samani and Valiantzas solar radiation models for FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration in Salem

Journal Madras Agricultural Journal 113(4–6), 198–210Year 2026Role First and corresponding authorAccepted 1 June 2026

The problem

Reference evapotranspiration is the quantity that irrigation scheduling turns on. The FAO-56 Penman–Monteith equation is the accepted method for estimating it — but it requires solar radiation as an input, and solar radiation is the one variable most Indian weather stations do not measure. Where a pyranometer is absent, radiation has to be estimated from what is recorded, usually temperature. The question is how much that substitution costs you.

What was done

Two estimation approaches were evaluated at Salem (Valapadi), Tamil Nadu: the temperature-based Hargreaves–Samani model and the Valiantzas formulation. Estimated radiation was fed through FAO-56 Penman–Monteith and the resulting ET₀ compared against the measured series.

  • Data: twenty-five years of NASA POWER records, 2000 to 2025.
  • Site: Salem (Valapadi), Tamil Nadu, India.
  • Comparison: estimated against measured ET₀, assessed on both magnitude and agreement.

Hargreaves–Samani reproduced measured ET₀ almost exactly on magnitude — 5.79 against 5.78 mm day⁻¹. Valiantzas gave the stronger statistical agreement, at R² = 0.982. The two results answer slightly different questions, and which model you prefer depends on whether you care more about the mean being right or about tracking variation through the season.

Why it matters

For a district planning irrigation without radiation instrumentation, this is the difference between scheduling on a defensible estimate and scheduling on habit. Both models are cheap to run on data that already exists, which is the point: the constraint in the field is rarely computation, it is instrumentation.
