A corrective action plan is not closed when the supplier says it is

Subject Social compliance audit follow-upMethodology SMETA/SEDEX 4-Pillar (7.0)Length 9 min read

A note on scope first, because the two names get used interchangeably and they are not the same thing. SMETA is the audit methodology; Sedex is the platform the results are shared on. A 2-Pillar audit covers labour standards and health and safety. The 4-Pillar adds environment and business ethics, and that is the scope assumed throughout this note.

The version of the SMETA process that lives in most people's heads is short: audit, finding, supplier uploads a document, closed. The real one has three more steps in it, and they are the ones that decide whether anything changed.

Finding → root cause → corrective action → implementation → evidence → verification → closure.

Correction is not corrective action

An auditor finds an emergency exit partially blocked. The response comes back: “removed the material.” That is a correction. It describes the immediate fix and nothing else, which means the same finding is available to be raised again next year.

Correction

Blocked exit → remove the boxes. The immediate problem is gone.

Corrective action

Why were boxes stored there? No designated storage area. Why not? Warehouse planning was inadequate. So: designated storage zones, visual marking, supervisor inspection, employee training.

What a real CAP answers

  • What happened? The finding, described plainly.
  • Why did it happen? The root cause, not the symptom.
  • What was corrected? The immediate action taken.
  • What system change prevents recurrence? The corrective action proper.
  • Who is responsible? A named person or department.
  • When will it be complete? A specific date.
  • What evidence will prove it? Objective, checkable evidence.

Worked example: excessive working hours

The finding is that workers exceeded applicable working-hour limits. The weak response — “workers were instructed not to work overtime” — fails because it never asks why the hours happened. Instruction is not a control when the production plan makes the overtime inevitable.

Plausible causes

  • Production planning that assumes overtime as normal capacity
  • Manpower shortage
  • Weak capacity planning
  • Late order changes pushed down without renegotiating the schedule
  • Supervisor practice
  • No monitoring of overtime as it accumulates

Corrective action

  • Revise production planning against realistic capacity
  • Establish an overtime approval route
  • Strengthen attendance monitoring
  • Create an escalation procedure when limits are approached
  • Train supervisors
  • Review working-hour trends periodically rather than at audit

Evidence

Attendance records, payroll records, the revised procedure, training records, management review minutes and monitoring reports. Note that this list is mostly records that already exist — the point of good evidence is rarely new paperwork.

Why evidence, specifically

An auditor cannot close a finding because someone says it has been corrected. The only usable response to an assertion is: show me. Depending on the finding, that means photographs, certificates, policies, procedures, attendance and wage records, inspection and training records, licences, invoices, maintenance reports or monitoring results.

Sedex describes desktop review as one route for verifying corrective-action evidence. Some findings cannot be closed that way at all — anything resting on worker testimony or on physical conditions may need a follow-up audit on site. Knowing which category a finding falls into before you promise a closure date saves a great deal of trouble.

Did it actually work?

The more demanding question is effectiveness over time. Fire extinguishers were inaccessible; they were moved. Still accessible a week later? Three months later? If yes, the organisation has evidence that the control holds, not just that the object was relocated on the day. That is the difference between a closed finding and a fixed problem.

A CAP structure worth using

ElementQuestion it answers
FindingWhat is wrong?
CorrectionWhat was immediately fixed?
Root causeWhy did it happen?
Corrective actionWhat system change is required?
OwnerWho is responsible?
DeadlineWhen will it be complete?
EvidenceWhat proves implementation?
VerificationWho checks effectiveness?
ClosureIs recurrence controlled?

What changed in 7.0

SMETA 7.0 took effect on 10 September 2024, replacing version 6.1, with follow-up audits under 6.1 continuing into the end of 2025. Two changes matter directly to how corrective action is handled.

  • The Management Systems Assessment. It replaces the former observations category with a structured review of how well a site's policies and procedures prevent non-compliance in the first place. In other words, the methodology itself moved toward root cause rather than symptom — which is the argument this note has been making throughout.
  • Collaborative Action Required findings. A category for issues a single supplier cannot resolve alone, such as living wages. It is an honest acknowledgement that some findings are not a failure of the audited site and cannot be closed by it.

The methodology also retains its cross-sector scope and the announced, semi-announced and unannounced audit types.

The practical rule

A finding is not closed by a document. It is closed by evidence that the underlying issue has been corrected and brought under control.

Which is worth holding onto when preparing a factory for a customer or 4-Pillar audit, because the preparation that actually works looks nothing like assembling a folder.
